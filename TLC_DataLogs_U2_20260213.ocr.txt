Observations of "marsh gas" trapped beneath lake ice
Recorder: E. H. Callister, Naturalist attached to a
coastal-interior survey party
Entry 1 - February 3, 1890 - Near the Copper River lowlands
The lake is locked in ice as thick as a man's forearm, yet
it is not plain white. In several bays the ice holds pale,
coin-like disks in stacks-bubbles arrested mid-rise, layered
like pressed flowers. The men call it "sleeping breath." I
have seen similar in temperate marsh ponds, but never in
such abundance. The air is sharp enough to split the lungs;
the water beneath must be starving for oxygen.

A trapper with us named K. says the bubbles burn. He speaks of a brief flare-blue at the heart-when the ice is disturbed and a flame is near. I did not encourage the demonstration. The idea of fire atop ice feels like a lie the world tells before it takes payment. Still, the claim aligns with what the older books call "marsh gas," the product of rot where air cannot go.

It is not witchcraft, only digestion without air. Vegetation and small fish settle into the black mud; the water above freezes, sealing the surface like a lid. In that oxygen-poor bottom, invisible life continues its work-slow, patient- producing a light gas that gathers in pockets and rises in beads until winter arrests it. The ice becomes a ledger: each bubble a date, each layer a season of decay.

Despite my reluctance, the men sought proof. A small crack was made near a bubble field. The release was sudden enough to startle, and one man-foolishly-brought a lantern too close. As daylight lengthens, the topmost bubbles soften and blur.
